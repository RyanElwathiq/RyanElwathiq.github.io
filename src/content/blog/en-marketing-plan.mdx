The moment a business owner decides to get serious, they ask for the same thing: a **marketing plan**. What usually arrives is a long document with a SWOT grid, a competitor breakdown, a content calendar covering a full year, and slides with confident arrows on them.

It gets read once. It gets filed. A month later nobody remembers a single line of it.

That is not a failure of the document. It is a document written to look serious, not to be executed on Monday morning.

<Callout title="The short version">
  A marketing plan that works fits on one page and answers six questions: who you are, who you are talking to, what is stopping the sale, what sets you apart, where you can reach people, and what you want in ninety days. **If you cannot start executing it this week, it is not a plan. It is a long essay about your business.**
</Callout>

## Why most marketing plans never get executed

Because they are written from the wrong end.

The usual plan starts at the top: vision, mission, annual objectives, market analysis. All of it respectable, none of it telling you what to do tomorrow morning. You finish the document understanding your situation better and still unable to take a single step.

A plan that works starts at the bottom. There is one knot in your business right now that is stopping the sale, and everything else hangs off it. If nobody knows you exist, a content calendar is premature. If people ask and then vanish, a bigger advertising budget will simply produce more people who ask and vanish.

There is a second reason: long plans try to cover every possibility, and possibilities eat time. Write twenty tasks and they all carry equal weight, which means none of them carry any. Write three moves for three months and you know exactly which one is late.

## Why these six questions specifically?

Because they are the smallest set you can build a decision on.

**Business type** decides the nature of the purchase. A product is bought in seconds, a service is sold after a conversation, and a clinic sells reassurance before it sells treatment. The same advert wins in one and dies in another for exactly this reason.

**Audience** decides the language and the location. **The knot** decides the priority, and it is the only question that tells you where to begin. **Your edge** decides the message, **channels** decide where it is executed, and **the goal** decides what you measure and what you stop.

You may notice there is no question about budget. That is deliberate: budget is the last decision, not the first. [Paid advertising](/services/paid-ads/) scales what works, and it scales the broken part just as loudly. Spending against an incomplete equation only accelerates the loss.

## Three mistakes that show up in nearly every plan

**One: goals that fight each other.** "I want a brand people remember, and I want sales this month." Both respectable, both pulling in opposite directions. Being remembered is slow, repetitive work that shows up months later. Fast sales need a sharp offer, urgency and quick replies. Chase both and you fund both while arriving at neither. Pick one for ninety days, and let the other follow it.

**Two: more channels than you can carry.** Five platforms, reluctant posting, and the same content recycled with no context. One channel done properly beats four done halfway. The test is simple: if you cannot post consistently on a channel for a month, it is not your channel yet.

**Three: a plan with no measurement.** Two months of effort, then the question "did it work?", and no number to answer it with. That is where the real danger starts, because now you decide by feel. This is why the most important line in the whole plan is two numbers written down weekly, even on paper stuck to the fridge. And if reading those numbers is the murky part, [the difference between a lead, a sale and ROAS](/signals/en-lead-sale-roas/) is worth twenty minutes of your time.

## Common questions about marketing plans

### How long should a marketing plan be?

One page is enough for a small or mid-sized business. Length signals nothing about seriousness. Usually it signals that whoever wrote it could not choose. Long documents earn their place in large organisations where teams need something written to agree on. You need something you can reread before publishing anything.

### What is the difference between a marketing plan and a strategy?

Strategy is the decision: who the audience is, where you stand, and what you will not do. The plan is the execution: what happens in month one, month two, and who does it. Strategy changes roughly once a year, the plan adjusts monthly against the numbers. Deciding the first is what [marketing strategy work](/services/marketing-strategy/) actually is: settling the decision before anyone opens a content calendar.

### How often should the plan be updated?

Review it monthly in a short sitting, and revise it seriously every three months. Any plan that survives a full year untouched means nobody is reading the numbers. Changing the plan is not indecision, it is the only evidence you are learning from the market.
