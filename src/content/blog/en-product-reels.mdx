There is one sentence I hear constantly, and every time it holds an entire business in place:

> "We have not started posting yet. We are waiting to shoot the products properly, we need a studio and a decent camera."

That wait costs more than the shoot ever would. While you are waiting for conditions to line up, the shop next door is posting every week with a phone, learning from every post, and building the habit of being seen.

Here is the useful part: the phone in your pocket right now out-shoots cameras that were considered professional ten years ago. The camera was never the problem.

<Callout title="The short version">
  What separates a good product photo from a cheap-looking one is not a more expensive lens. It is **clean light, a quiet background and deliberate arrangement**. Your phone and a window do most of the job. A studio solves specific problems (reflective surfaces, jewellery, food), and outside those cases you are paying for something you could do today.

## The camera is not the problem, the light is

Take any product photo that feels amateurish and look at it closely. You will usually find the same three symptoms: yellow shadows from the ceiling light, a background containing a sofa and a remote control, and the product sitting at a slightly random angle.

None of those get fixed by a better camera. All of them get fixed by arrangement.

Your phone's sensor is clever and covers for a lot on its own, but there is one thing it cannot invent: light that is not there. When light is low, it raises sensitivity, and you get grain and washed-out colour. That is exactly the texture that makes an image read as cheap.

## Professional-looking product photography with four things you already own

You do not need to buy anything to start. These four do the work:

**The window.** The largest source of soft light you will ever get for free. Put the table beside it so light arrives from the side of the product rather than from behind your phone. Side light reveals texture and shape; light coming from behind the camera flattens everything. Avoid direct sun, which creates hard shadows and blown-out patches. An overcast day is the best shooting day of the year.

**The backdrop.** A large sheet of white card curved from the table up the wall gives you a seamless background with no visible join. If you want warmth instead, a wooden board or a pressed linen cloth reads more premium than any glossy surface. One rule only: the backdrop never competes with the product, in colour or in pattern.

**The bounce.** The side facing away from the window will fall dark. Put a piece of white card (an A4 sheet works) on that side to push some light back. This move costs nothing and changes the photo more than any filter will.

**Something to hold the phone still.** A small tripod, or a stack of books. Stability lets you shoot in less light without shake, and it keeps every product at the same height and angle, which is what makes a feed look organised rather than assembled.

And the light tent, or lightbox? It earns its place in exactly one situation: small items that need the same clean white background repeated a hundred times over (accessories, small bottles, spare parts). Bear in mind that it lights from every direction at once, so there are almost no shadows, and no shadows means the images feel a little lifeless. For photos that need some character, the window wins.

## Five shots are enough for any product

Not every photo does the same job. This is the smallest set that actually covers you:

- **The clean shot.** Product alone on a plain background. This is the shop and catalogue image, and its job is clarity, not beauty.
- **The detail shot.** Stitching, texture, closure, ingredients. This is the trust shot, because it says you are not afraid of a close look.
- **The scale shot.** The product next to something everyone can size by eye, a hand or a mug. It answers half your inbox before anyone asks.
- **The in-use shot.** The product doing its job. Customers do not buy the object, they buy the moment they use it.
- **The atmosphere shot.** The product inside a scene that resembles the customer's life. This is the social shot, and its job is to stop the thumb, not to explain.

The first shot sells on your store page, the last one sells on social. Using the same image in both places quietly loses you one of them.

## From the same session: reels with no equipment

The photos are done and the set is still standing. This is the part most people skip.

**The first two seconds decide everything.** Nobody is watching, they are passing through. If your opening frame is a logo or a wide shot of the shop, they are already gone. Start from the result or from the problem: the moment the box opens, the stain before it disappears, the thing that makes the eye pause. The logical order (intro, then presentation, then conclusion) is the order of meetings, not of reels.

It is also the hardest part of the whole reel, and the part most often written in a hurry. **Cut to the beat.** Load the music before you start cutting and place every cut on a beat. Viewers feel the difference even when they cannot name it. Keep each shot short: if a shot adds no new information after a second and a half, cut it.

**Captions are not optional.** Most people watch with the sound off, on a bus, at a desk, next to someone asleep. If the message lives in the audio, the message did not arrive. Put clear text on screen, large, and positioned where the app's own buttons will not cover it. Make the first line appear within the first second, because that is what stops the thumb, not the music.

**Shoot vertical from the start.** Cropping a horizontal clip to vertical throws away half the frame and usually cuts the product in two.

And when you reach the point where you have plenty of footage and no time, [editing](/services/video-editing/) becomes someone else's job, and [organising and scheduling the content](/services/social-media/) becomes a management decision rather than a matter of taste.

## The honest part: when you genuinely need a studio

Not everything can be solved by a window. In these cases a studio is not a luxury:

- **Reflective products.** Glass, stainless steel, metal and screens mirror everything in the room, including you and your phone. Controlling that needs full command of the lighting and the reflections, which is precisely what a studio buys you.
- **Jewellery and watches.** Small pieces with fine detail need macro optics and considered lighting. It is a specialism of its own, and it is not phone work.
- **Food at large sizes.** A dish photographed for a menu board or a billboard needs lighting plus a stylist, because food dies visually within minutes.
- **Large catalogues in a single day.** When eighty items must come out at identical angles under identical light, mechanical consistency saves days of work.
- **The hero campaign.** An image that will be printed large or carried by a serious ad budget deserves genuine professional photography, because every flaw in it scales with the money behind it.

Outside those cases, honestly: shoot it on your phone. And if your business is early and your products are simple, you may not need me at this stage, or a photographer either. You need to publish.

## Product photography costs: why there is no number in this article

I do not put figures here, because any number written before seeing your business is a guess, and you would build the wrong expectation on it.

What I can tell you is what to compare on: **volume** (how many products and how many shots each, not how many hours), **retouching** (do the images arrive finished or raw), **usage rights** (can you use them in paid ads and on your store, or social only), and **revision rounds** (how many rounds of notes before extra work is charged). Almost every dispute I have seen happens on those four points rather than on the price itself.

## Common questions about shooting on a phone

### Which phone should I use?

The one you have. Any phone from the last four years produces images good enough for social and for a store page. If you change one setting, open the camera options, enable the highest available resolution, and switch off any beauty processing.

### Does the background have to be white?

No. White matters for store and catalogue images, because it makes everything look consistent. On social, a coloured or wooden surface often works better, as long as it stays one calm tone and does not pull attention from the product.

### Should I use the flash?

No. A phone flash fires hard light from the same direction as the lens, which flattens the product and drops a harsh shadow behind it. If the light is low, move the shoot to tomorrow morning instead of switching the flash on.

### How many photos does one product need?

Five, as long as they do different jobs. Twenty versions of the same angle will not add a single sale, but they will consume the hour you could have spent on the next product.

### Do I need a microphone for reels?

For most product videos, no, because the original audio gets replaced by music or by a voice-over recorded separately. A microphone becomes necessary the moment you appear on camera and speak.

## If you started tomorrow

You do not need to buy anything. You need a window, a clean table, a sheet of white card, an hour of your time, and a five-shot list written before you begin.

Shoot one product that way, put it beside its old photo, and judge with your own eyes. If the difference is obvious, you have your method for the rest of the range. If you are still not convinced, you at least now recognise exactly what is missing before you pay anyone.
